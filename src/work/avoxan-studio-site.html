Case file 04 · Our own website

How we built avoxan.com.

A studio's own site is the one project with no client to blame. So here is every decision behind the October 2026 rebuild, what we traded for it, and what we'd still change. If you're hiring a studio, this is how we think.

The brief

Stop describing the work. Show it.

Our first site was written for a studio with no clients yet, so it leaned on promises: guarantees, an itemized offer, a lot of reassurance. By autumn 2026 we had three finished projects and three clients willing to say on camera what working with us was like.

The new site had one job: let that work and those voices carry the argument. Everything else, from the type to the build, had to get out of their way.

  • Show each project in its own light, not framed in our brand.
  • Let clients speak in their own words, unedited.
  • Prove the AI product with a real recording, not a mockup.
  • Publish every price, down to the add-ons.

Decision log

Ten calls, and what each cost.

Each entry is written the way we write them for clients: what we decided, why, and the trade-off we accepted.

  1. D-01

    A screening room, not a portfolio grid

    Decision. As each case study scrolls into view, the whole page takes on that client's own colors: Bag Chasers' gold on black, Clever's green on cream, thePrepDoc's rose on plum.

    Why. A thumbnail grid flattens every project into our style. Taking over the page shows the work the way its customers see it.

    Trade-off. Every color on the site has to read through five live tokens, so no section can hard-code a color. It's stricter to build, and worth it.

  2. D-02

    Instrument Serif, Geist and JetBrains Mono

    Decision. One display serif used large, one clean sans for reading, and a monospace only for small labels.

    Why. Instrument Serif's condensed shapes let a headline run huge without wrapping into a wall. Our old serif, Fraunces, was lovely but wide, and more than three times the file size.

    Trade-off. Instrument Serif has one weight. Hierarchy comes from size and italics, never bold.

  3. D-03

    Ember on warm black, with one light page

    Decision. We kept our ember accent and warm ink ground, and dropped the light-mode toggle. The only light page is for med spas.

    Why. Dark makes client screenshots glow. But a med spa owner should land in a world that feels like their treatment rooms, so that page is porcelain and clay.

    Trade-off. Visitors who prefer light mode don't get a choice on most pages.

  4. D-04

    Clients, on camera and unedited

    Decision. The homepage plays short clips from our Zoom calls with clients, and an “overheard” band runs other things they said, without a name on every line.

    Why. A polished testimonial reads like marketing. A client thinking out loud on a call reads like the truth.

    Trade-off. Video is heavy, so clips load only when someone presses play.

  5. D-05

    A real call, not a mockup

    Decision. The AI receptionist page plays an actual 79-second recording. The transcript follows along, and a lead card fills in as the AI collects each detail.

    Why. Everyone selling AI shows a mockup. Hearing it handle a leaking sink at night settles the “does it sound robotic?” question in a minute.

    Trade-off. The transcript and timings had to be logged by hand against the audio.

  6. D-06

    Every price, itemized

    Decision. The pricing page lists everything: each website deliverable with its standalone value, all three AI plans, every add-on and the price we'll charge next.

    Why. Owners hate booking a call to learn a price. AI assistants also recommend businesses whose prices they can read.

    Trade-off. We can't quote different people different numbers. That's the point.

  7. D-07

    Hand-coded, with a 300-line build

    Decision. No framework and no page builder. Each page is plain HTML; a small Python script adds the shared header, footer and structured data, then Cloudflare Pages serves static files.

    Why. Nothing to update, nothing to patch, and pages that load fast on a phone in a parking lot.

    Trade-off. Editing means editing code. Fine for us; it's why we usually build clients on Webflow or WordPress instead.

  8. D-08

    Structured data that can't drift

    Decision. Every page carries JSON-LD: services and offers, how-to steps, articles, the demo call with its transcript. FAQ schema is generated from the questions you can see on the page.

    Why. Search engines and AI assistants quote what they can parse. Generating it from the page means the answers and the data always match.

    Trade-off. Every FAQ answer has to be written to stand on its own, out of context.

  9. D-09

    Motion with manners

    Decision. Text and images ease in as you scroll, but only when JavaScript runs and the visitor hasn't asked their device for reduced motion. If the script fails, the page shows everything after three seconds.

    Why. Animation should never be the reason someone can't read a page.

    Trade-off. Every animated element needs a complete, static fallback.

  10. D-10

    Our own chat, as a live demo

    Decision. “Ask Avoxan” answers questions about pricing and process on every page, and waits until you scroll before it appears.

    Why. We sell website chat to clients, so our site should run it.

    Trade-off. It's an AI and can be wrong, so it says so, and links to a real call for anything binding.

Still on the list

What we'd still change.

  • Short case videos for each project, cut from the full client calls.
  • Spanish versions of the AI receptionist pages, since the product already speaks Spanish.
  • Real before-and-after numbers for each client, once they've had six months on the new sites.
  • Retiring the founding-rate banner once our first ten AI clients are signed.
